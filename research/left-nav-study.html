Left Rail Study

The nav as a vertical bar down the left instead of a horizontal bar across the top, rendered with the app's own stylesheet in both themes. Each specimen is a whole Next actions screen, because moving the nav is not a change to the nav — it is a change to how much room everything else gets.

Width is the whole argument against a rail; the g hints are the argument for one.
A

Top bar — as built today

The baseline
Dark
Light

Fourteen targets in one wrapping row. It costs 3rem of height, always, and at Narrow it wraps to a second row and costs 5.4rem — on the axis a list of items can least afford to lose. Press Hold g: the counts blank, because the jump letters and the corner pills want the same strip of space. That workaround is the shipped answer from nav-badge-study.html, and it exists only because the bar is horizontal.

B

Rail, straight translation

Same thirteen, stacked
Dark
Light

The same order, nothing regrouped. Two things fall out for free. The counts get a right edge of their own, so they can sit inline where a sidebar count belongs instead of hanging off a corner. And the jump hints get a permanent left gutter — press Hold g and nothing has to blank. The row under the cursor is also unambiguous now: the active view is a filled bar at a fixed x, which matters more here than in most apps, because no view has an h1 — the nav is the only place the page says its name.

C

Rail, grouped

The thing a top bar cannot do
Dark
Light

Vertical space is cheap, so the thirteen destinations can admit that they are five different kinds of place: where things arrive, what you do today, what you have committed to, what is deferred, and the machinery. A top bar can only put them in a row and let adjacency imply it. The cost is four captions' worth of height and one more decision to keep true — a view that moves between groups is now a doc change, not just a reorder.

C, with Archive, Audit and Settings pushed to the bottom of the rail. It is the same split the key bar already makes between the keys for this view and the keys that are always there: the ten places you work, and the three you look things up in. It also gives the rail a reason to run the full height, which puts the key bar's left edge in line with the content it describes rather than under the nav.

What the rail costs, in numbers

Main is centred by padding and caps its column at 62rem. The rail is 11.5rem. So the arithmetic is not "a rail is narrower", it is a threshold:

≥ 76remWindow wide enough that the column was already capped — the rail costs nothing, it eats margin that was empty anyway, and buys back 3rem of height.
48–76remThe laptop case. The rail takes 11.5rem straight off the reading column. Rows wrap sooner; the filter bar gains a line.
< 48remA phone. 11.5rem is a quarter of the screen and the rail is unusable — the top bar wraps and survives. A rail needs an answer here that today's bar does not.

Try the three width buttons above on card A and card D. On Desktop the rail is free. On Laptop it is a real trade: height for width. On Narrow it does not work, and that is the one thing that has to be decided before any of this ships.

What it breaks

The nav badge decision. nav-badge-study settled on the outlined corner pill, and half its reasoning was about a horizontal bar: labels shifting sideways as counts change, and the collision with the g hints. In a rail neither pressure exists — rows are fixed-width, so an inline right-aligned count moves nothing, and the hints have their own gutter. Moving the nav reopens that decision; it does not override it silently.

The keybar's alignment. Today it spans the window and centres its padding the same way main does. With a full-height rail it starts after the rail, so the two left edges agree. That is better, but it is a second change riding along with the first.

The + button. A rail row is wide enough to carry a word beside the plus. Left as just + here on purpose — that is a separate call, not part of turning the bar.

The recommendation

D, if the app is used at a desktop width. Above 76rem the rail is free — it spends margin that was already empty and hands back height to the list, which is the axis every view in this app is short of. It also dissolves two workarounds rather than adding any: the blanking counts and the cornered badge.

The narrow window is the blocker, not the design. The honest options are to collapse the rail to a top bar below a breakpoint (two navs to keep true), or to decide the app is a desktop app and say so in design.md. The second is cheaper and probably truer, but it is a design decision and belongs in the docs before any CSS moves.
